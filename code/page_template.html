How many names on your war memorial?

The Imperial War Museums register lists {{register.rows|int}} war memorials across the United Kingdom. I counted, place by place, how many First World War dead each community's memorial names, and set that against how many people live there today.

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · November 2026 · Data: Imperial War Museums, War Memorials Register; Wikidata

Almost every town and village in Britain has one: a cross on the green, a board inside the church door, a list of names that someone reads aloud each November. The Imperial War Museums' War Memorials Register has been recording them since 1989. It now holds {{register.rows|int}} entries, from Boer War obelisks to a single name added to a family gravestone.

I wanted to use it to answer a simple question for every place at once: how many names does your memorial carry, and is that a lot for a place your size? Doing that honestly means dealing with a problem anyone who has looked at a memorial knows well. The same man is often named three or four times: on the village cross, on the board in his church, on his old school's roll and on the plaque at the works where he had a job. So I picked one community memorial per place and never added memorials together.

This is a companion to my earlier study of Thankful Villages, the handful of parishes from which every serving man came home. Here they become the far tail of a much larger picture.

    02 · The registerCrosses, boards and gravestones

    Most entries are not crosses on village greens. The commonest kind is a board, plaque or tablet ({{type_groups.0.n|int}}), and the second commonest is a name added to a family gravestone ({{type_groups.1.n|int}}): a son buried in France, remembered at home. Rolls of honour and books of remembrance come next ({{type_groups.2.n|int}}), then crosses ({{type_groups.3.n|int}}) and church windows ({{type_groups.4.n|int}}).

    03 · Counting each man onceThe double-counting problem

    Add up every First World War memorial in the register and you get {{names.ww1_dead|int}} names of the dead. The usual figure for the United Kingdom's military dead and missing from the British Isles is about {{benchmark.uk_dead|int}}. So, on average, each man appears about {{benchmark.entries_per_man|x1}} times. Some of that comes from {{names.ww1_big|int}} huge regimental, national and county rolls that hold {{names.ww1_big_dead|int}} names between them. Leave those out and it is still about {{benchmark.entries_per_man_excl_big|x1}} names per man.

    Where the First World War names sit
    Names of the dead on First World War memorials, by the kind of body that put the memorial up, as I classified them from each memorial's title and type.

    That is why I never add memorials together. Instead I group the register's memorials into places: those that share a town or village name and lie within 6 km of each other. That gives {{places.n|int}} places. For each place I take a single memorial: the community memorial (a cross, cenotaph, board or roll put up by the village or town) that names the most First World War dead. If a place has none, I take its church memorial with the most names, which in many small villages is the parish's own memorial. Memorials to one person, to a regiment, a school, a works, a club, or to a whole county are never chosen.

    The effect is large. Summed over every memorial, places carry {{places.naive_sum|int}} names; one memorial per place brings that to {{places.chosen_sum|int}}, {{places.naive_over_chosen|x1}} times fewer. That second figure is close to the {{benchmark.uk_dead|int}} dead, but I would not read much into the match: some places still overlap (a town memorial can include its villages), and many places have no count at all.

    04 · How many namesA typical memorial carries {{size_median|int}} names

    Of the {{places.with_ww1|int}} places with a First World War count, {{size_n|int}} have a community or church memorial that records names of the dead. The median is {{size_median|int}} names. Most are short lists: {{size_dist.1.n|int}} memorials have 5 to 9 names and {{size_dist.2.n|int}} have 10 to 19. At the other end, {{size_dist.7.n|int}} places have a memorial with a thousand names or more, all of them cities and large towns.

    05 · Per 1,000 residentsHow heavy were the losses?

    A long list in a city is not the same as a long list in a hamlet. To compare places I divide the names by the number of people who live there now. That is the weak point of this study, and I want to be plain about it: what we need is the population in 1911, and for almost every place I only have a modern one, from Wikidata, matched by name. Places that have grown since 1914 will look as if they lost few men; places that have shrunk will look as if they lost many.

    With that warning, here is the picture for the {{rate.n|int}} villages and small towns of {{rate.lo|int}} to {{rate.hi|int}} residents with an exact count on their memorial. The median memorial carries {{rate.median|f1}} names for every 1,000 people living there today. The middle half runs from {{rate.p25|f1}} to {{rate.p75|f1}}. Pooled together, they name {{rate.pooled|f1}} per 1,000, almost exactly the {{benchmark.per1000|f1}} per 1,000 you get from {{benchmark.uk_dead|int}} dead in a population of {{benchmark.uk_pop|m1}} million.

    The modern population drags the numbers around. The smaller a place is today, the higher its rate: a median of {{rate_by_pop.0.median|f1}} per 1,000 for places of {{rate_by_pop.0.band}} people, falling to {{rate_by_pop.4.median|f1}} for places of {{rate_by_pop.4.band}}. Some of that will be real, but much of it is growth and decline since 1911 that I cannot see.

    A better test is possible for a few places. Wikidata holds a 1911 census population for {{rate1911.n|int}} of them. Against 1911 residents their median is {{rate1911.median|f1}} names per 1,000; against today's residents the same places give {{rate1911.median_modern|f1}}. The memorials name about twice as many men, per head of the 1911 village, as the national rate would suggest. My guess is that village memorials cast a wide net, naming men born there who had moved away and men from outlying farms and hamlets, but that sample is small and not chosen at random.

    Scotland's long lists

    Scottish places stand out. Their median is {{rate_by_nation.0.median|f1}} names per 1,000 residents today, against {{rate_by_nation.1.median|f1}} in England and {{rate_by_nation.3.median|f1}} in Wales. The Highland area, Dumfries and Galloway and the Scottish Borders top the county list. It is tempting to read that as heavier Scottish losses, and it may partly be. But I cannot separate it from two other things that push the same way: rural places that have lost people since 1911 look worse on a modern count, and a Scottish memorial often serves a whole parish, larger than the village whose name it carries. Wikipedia's list of Thankful Villages notes that none has been identified in Scotland. Welsh places sit lowest, and they are also the biggest in this set: a median of {{rate_by_nation.3.median_pop|int}} residents, against {{rate_by_nation.1.median_pop|int}} in England and {{rate_by_nation.0.median_pop|int}} in Scotland.

    The highest rates
    Places of {{highest_rule.min_pop|int}} or more residents today, with a single-place memorial (titles naming several parishes, a district, an estate or a county are left out). Treat these as leads: a high rate can mean heavy losses, a place that has shrunk, or a memorial that also names men from round about.

    06 · The Second World WarOne name for every three

    Many memorials carry both wars. For the {{ww2.n|int}} community memorials with exact counts for both, the median memorial has {{ww2.median_ratio|x2}} Second World War names for every First World War name: about one for every three. Only {{ww2.more_ww2|int}} ({{ww2.more_ww2_share|pct1}}) list more names for 1939 to 1945 than for 1914 to 1918.

    07 · Thankful VillagesThe end of the tail

    A Thankful Village is one from which everyone who served in the First World War came home. My earlier study used the list of {{thankful.n|int}} villages on Wikipedia ({{thankful.england|int}} in England and {{thankful.wales|int}} in Wales, {{thankful.doubly|int}} of them flagged as doubly thankful because they also lost no one in the Second World War). In the register I found {{thankful.matched|int}} of them. In {{thankful.with_served_roll|int}} the register holds a roll of the men who served and came back, a median of {{thankful.served_median|int}} names each, and {{thankful.thank_title|int}} have a memorial with "thankful" or "thanksgiving" in its title. The rest have no memorial in the register within a few kilometres, or only a memorial with no count.

    Four have something more. At {{thankful_list.16.village}} the church board is recorded with {{thankful.nether_kellet_dead|int}} names of men who died, exactly the {{earlier.nether_kellet_served|int}} who served according to the village's Wikipedia article, as used in my earlier study, which looks like a slip in the coding of the record. At three others (East Norton, Chantry and Tellisford) the only names of the dead are on memorials to one or two people. The register cannot say whether those men counted as villagers; Wikipedia's list notes that doubts are "generally over the place of residence of a serviceman".

    Among {{tail.n|int}} small places with an exact count, only {{tail.zero|int}} have a community record with no dead at all.

    The Thankful Villages are the far end of the distribution above. Of the {{tail.n|int}} villages and small towns with an exact count, {{tail.zero|int}} ({{tail.zero_share|pct1}}) have a community or church record that lists only men who served, with no dead; {{tail.zero_tv|int}} of those are Thankful Villages. Another {{tail.one|int}} name one man and {{tail.two|int}} name two. Even the lightest losses were almost never zero.

    The register also points to places worth a second look. Across all sizes there are {{served_only.n|int}} places where the only First World War community or church record is a roll of those who served, and no memorial at the place records any dead. {{served_only.tv|int}} are Thankful Villages. Of the others, {{candidates.n|int}} are small places with up to 100 names on the roll. They are not new Thankful Villages: a roll of service can sit beside a memorial the register has not recorded, or one filed under a neighbouring parish's name. But they are where I would start looking.

    My earlier study found that chance alone, with each serving man facing a {{earlier.risk|pct}} risk of death, would predict somewhere between {{earlier.expected_low|int}} and {{earlier.expected_high|int}} Thankful Villages in England and Wales, far more than the {{thankful.n|int}} known. This register explains part of the gap from the other side: village memorials name more men, for their size, than a simple count of residents would suggest. A memorial that reaches out to men born in the parish, or living just over its boundary, makes it much harder for any village to have no one to name.

    How I did this

    Data. The Imperial War Museums' War Memorials Register, read through its public data interface on {{register.retrieved}}: {{register.rows|int}} memorials (the interface reported {{register.expected|int}}), each with its title, types, lost flag, locations and, for each war, the number of names, how many died and how many served, and whether the count is exact. {{register.no_location|int}} have no coordinates. I placed each memorial at its current location and took its county and nation from the nearest Ordnance Survey Open Names settlement (Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands from simple boxes).

    Names of the dead. The number recorded as died; where neither died nor served is given but there are names, the number of names. A memorial with only served names counts as a roll of service, with no dead.

    Kinds of memorial. I gave each memorial one class from its type and title: to one person (a gravestone addition, a serviceman figure, a single name, or a title that starts with a rank or initials), military (a regiment, ship, squadron, aircraft crash or veterans' body), regional (a county, national or clan memorial), school, workplace (firms, railways, banks, the Post Office, police, clubs and societies), church (churches, chapels, congregations, windows and church fittings) and community (the rest). These rules are simple and will misfile some memorials. Memorials to one named person ({{classes.individual.n|int}}) are counted but their titles are not published.

    Places and the community memorial. Memorials sharing a town name within 6 km of each other form a place. For each place I take the community memorial with the most First World War dead; failing that, the church memorial with the most; a roll of service only when no memorial at the place records any dead. The same is done separately for the Second World War.

    Population. A Wikidata settlement with the same name within 5 km of the place, preferring civil parishes and Welsh communities. Wikidata stores a modern figure, mostly from the 2011 or 2021 census. Rates use places of {{rate.lo|int}} to {{rate.hi|int}} residents with an exact count. It is a proxy, and it is the biggest weakness here.

    Known problems. The register is built by volunteers and is not complete: many memorials have no count yet, and a count can be wrong. Big-city figures are often rounded. Town names in the register vary (a suburb or a parish name), so a place here can be a parish, a village or part of a town. The Thankful Villages and their coordinates come from my earlier study, which took them from Wikipedia.
