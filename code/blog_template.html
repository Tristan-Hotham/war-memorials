Britain has more than a hundred thousand war memorials. I counted, place by place, how many First World War names each community's memorial carries, and set that against the size of the place. Almost no village is without a name to read.

Every November someone in almost every town and village in Britain stands by a cross, a cenotaph or a board in the church and reads out a list of names. I have always wondered how long those lists are, and whether the length tells you anything about the place. The Imperial War Museums' War Memorials Register makes it possible to ask that question everywhere at once. It records {{register.rows|int}} memorials, and for many of them it counts the names.

This is a companion to my earlier post on Thankful Villages, the handful of parishes from which every serving man came home. Here they turn out to be the very end of a long tail.

One man, many memorials

Add up every First World War memorial in the register and you get {{names.ww1_dead|int}} names of the dead. The usual figure for the dead and missing from the British Isles is about {{benchmark.uk_dead|int}}. So each man is named about {{benchmark.entries_per_man|x1}} times on average: on his village cross, on the board in his church, on his school's roll, at the works where he had a job and in his regiment's book of remembrance.

So I never add memorials together. I grouped the register into {{places.n|int}} places, memorials that share a town or village name and sit within 6 km of each other, and for each place took the single community memorial with the most names, or the church memorial where there was none. In Buckden in Cambridgeshire that means the {{examples.Buckden.dead|int}} names on the village memorial, rather than the {{examples.Buckden.dead_all|int}} you get by adding up all {{examples.Buckden.n_mem|int}} of its memorials. Across the country the total falls from {{places.naive_sum|int}} to {{places.chosen_sum|int}}.

A typical list

Most memorials are short lists. Of the {{size_n|int}} places whose memorial records names of the dead, the median memorial carries {{size_median|int}}. {{size_dist.2.n|int}} have 10 to 19 names. At the other end, {{size_dist.7.n|int}} cities and large towns have a memorial with a thousand or more, led by Glasgow's roll of honour with {{biggest.0.dead|int}}.

How heavy was the loss?

Fifty names means something different in a hamlet and in a town, so I divided each list by the number of people who live in the place, taken from Wikidata. I want to be clear about the weakness here: what we really need is the population in 1911, and for almost every place I only have today's. A village that has doubled in size since 1914 will look as if it lost few men.

With that caution, for {{rate.n|int}} villages and small towns of {{rate.lo|int}} to {{rate.hi|int}} people with an exact count, the median memorial carries {{rate.median|f1}} names for every 1,000 people living there today. The middle half runs from {{rate.p25|f1}} to {{rate.p75|f1}}. Pooled together, it comes to {{rate.pooled|f1}} per 1,000, close to the national {{benchmark.per1000|f1}}.

The modern population pushes the numbers about. Places of {{rate_by_pop.0.band}} people today have a median of {{rate_by_pop.0.median|f1}} names per 1,000; places of {{rate_by_pop.4.band}} have {{rate_by_pop.4.median|f1}}. For the {{rate1911.n|int}} places where Wikidata does hold a 1911 census figure, the median against 1911 residents is {{rate1911.median|f1}} per 1,000, about twice the national rate. My reading is that village memorials cast a wide net, naming men born there who had moved away and men from the farms round about, but that sample is small.

Scotland's long lists

Scottish places stand out, with a median of {{rate_by_nation.0.median|f1}} names per 1,000 residents against {{rate_by_nation.1.median|f1}} in England and {{rate_by_nation.3.median|f1}} in Wales. The Highland area, Dumfries and Galloway and the Scottish Borders top the county list. Some of that may be heavier losses, but I cannot separate it from depopulation since 1911 and from Scottish memorials that serve a whole parish. Welsh places sit lowest, and they are also the largest places in the set.

The end of the tail

Of the {{thankful.n|int}} Thankful Villages on Wikipedia's list, I found {{thankful.matched|int}} in the register. In {{thankful.with_served_roll|int}} the register holds a roll of the men who served and came back, a median of {{thankful.served_median|int}} names each. At Nether Kellet the church board is coded with {{thankful.nether_kellet_dead|int}} names of men who died, exactly the number who served according to the village's Wikipedia article, which looks like a slip in the record.

Against the whole country, the Thankful Villages are very rare indeed. Of {{tail.n|int}} villages and small towns with an exact count, only {{tail.zero|int}} have a community record that lists no dead, and {{tail.zero_tv|int}} of those are Thankful Villages. {{tail.one|int}} name a single man.

The register also turns up {{candidates.n|int}} other small places where the only First World War record is a roll of the men who served and nothing at the place records any dead. They are not new Thankful Villages: a memorial may simply be missing from the register or filed under a neighbouring name. But they are where I would start looking, and they are listed on the interactive page.

That helps with the puzzle in my earlier post, where chance alone predicted hundreds more Thankful Villages than are known. If a village's memorial reaches out to men born there and men from round about, far more men were at risk for each village than its size suggests, and coming through with no names at all becomes much harder.

How I did this

The memorial data is the Imperial War Museums' War Memorials Register, read through its public data interface on {{register.retrieved}}. I gave each memorial a class (community, church, school, workplace, military, regional or to one person) from its type and title, grouped memorials into places by town name and distance, and took one community memorial per place. Populations are modern figures from Wikidata, matched by name within 5 km, mostly civil parishes. The national figure of {{benchmark.uk_dead|int}} dead and missing comes from Wikipedia's article on First World War casualties. I publish counts and memorial titles, never names of the dead, and leave out the titles of memorials to a single person.

The register is built by volunteers and is not complete, many memorials have no count, and my rules for classing memorials will misfile some. Treat the rates as a guide to what memorials record, not a measure of each community's true losses. Use the interactive version to find your own memorial and follow the link to its full record.
